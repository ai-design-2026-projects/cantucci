\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.} The evaluation ran against a compact catalogue of \textbf{288 films}. The
pipeline scales to the full catalogue of tens of thousands of titles, but a small catalogue keeps
whole-catalogue operations, oracle inspection, and the model context window tractable within the
turn budget while remaining large enough for meaningful structure to emerge.

\paragraph{Conditions.} Two conditions were compared. The \textbf{conversational} condition is
the full multi-agent system of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}. The \textbf{baseline} is a
\emph{single-prompt} system: one model call per turn receives the film list, the current cluster
state, and the transcript, and emits the entire resulting grouping, the declared operation, and
the reply in one response, with no embeddings, density clustering, concept axes, clarifier, or
labeling agent. The baseline therefore isolates whether the modular, embedding-grounded pipeline
buys anything over a single capable model doing everything from text.

\paragraph{Models.} The system, oracle, and judge run on three different model families
(Table~\ref{tab:models}). Within the system, the stronger model handles intent parsing, concept
construction, and explanation, while the faster model handles labeling, clarification, and
suggestions.

\paragraph{Fixed parameters.} Text and review are encoded at 1024 dimensions with a
sentence-embedding model and the trailer with a vision-language model; fusion weights are
$0.6/0.4$ for text-review and text-trailer alike. Density clustering uses a minimum cluster size
of $50$ on the base catalogue and $10$ for in-conversation splits. The intent confidence threshold
is $0.6$, the turn budget is $15$ oracle turns, and at most five exemplar titles are shown per
cluster in the oracle's view. Per-session cost ceilings are \$10 for the system and \$2 each for
the oracle and the judge. All model calls use a fixed decoding seed; sessions span a shared set of
personas and ground truths, with the conversational condition seeded over $\{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$ and
the baseline over $\{1,2,5,7\}$.

\paragraph{Procedure.} For each persona/seed pair a session is created and driven by the oracle
through the same code path as a live client until it stops or exhausts the budget; deterministic
metrics and the seven judge dimensions are then computed and stored. This yielded \textbf{35
conversational} sessions (29 oracle-rated) and \textbf{30 baseline} sessions (27 rated). All
figures below are extracted directly from the resulting database and use the confidence interval
of Equation~\ref{eq:ci}.

\subsection{Quantitative Results}

The headline is that the conversational system is judged to \emph{do what the oracle asks}
markedly better than the baseline, while the baseline's apparent advantages are tied to it
producing almost no structure at all.

On \textbf{operation appropriateness} the conversational system scores $3.94\pm0.31$ against the
baseline's $2.73\pm0.35$, the only judge dimension whose intervals are disjoint, and the clearest
result in the study (Table~\ref{tab:judge}). It also trends higher on intent alignment
($3.09\pm0.43$ vs.\ $2.33\pm0.47$) and on the oracle's own rating ($3.41\pm0.49$ vs.\
$2.82\pm0.64$). The baseline scores higher on label accuracy and clustering coherence, but these
are confounded: as Table~\ref{tab:effort} shows, the baseline ends with $1.97\pm0.53$ clusters and
never triggered a clarifier, i.e.\ it mostly returns one or two coarse groups that are easy to
label coherently. Three judge dimensions have no baseline counterpart, namely explanation quality,
suggestion meaningfulness, and concept-axis quality, because the single-prompt system cannot
produce explanations, proactive suggestions, or concept axes at all.

Two further results stand out. First, the \textbf{cost of going multi-agent is negligible}: at
\$0.0126 vs.\ \$0.0123 per turn (and \$0.169 vs.\ \$0.153 per session) the conversational system
is no more expensive than the single call, because roughly a quarter of its turns are cheap
non-operational moves such as clarify, explain, and small-talk; this also explains why it executes
fewer operations per turn ($0.74$ vs.\ $0.93$). Second, the conversational system
\textbf{completes the oracle's intended trajectory more often and misbehaves less}: it finishes
satisfied in $49\%$ of sessions and is stopped for misbehaviour in $34\%$, whereas the baseline
finishes in $37\%$ and is stopped for misbehaviour in the majority: $53\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:termination}).

\subsection{Qualitative Results}

Two sessions illustrate the behaviour behind the numbers.

\paragraph{Success (\texttt{worldcinema} persona, oracle rating 5).} The oracle's goal was to
explore language diversity, then narrow to French cinema and separate personal from societal
narratives. The system handled the structured parts well, but the session also exposes a loop and
an unhonoured cluster count, yet was still rated 5.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Oracle:} split the entire collection by its original language.\\
\textbf{System:} Partitioned 288 movies by original language into 13 groups.\\
\textbf{Oracle:} zoom in on the \textsf{fr} cluster.\\
\textbf{System:} Focused on \textsf{fr}: 7 movies retained, all others discarded.\\
\textbf{Oracle:} undo, then split the \emph{entire} catalogue by language again.\\
\textbf{System:} Partitioned 7 movies by original language into 1 group.
  \quad\textit{[loop: only the 7 focused films remained, not the full catalogue]}\\
\textbf{Oracle:} we've run into a loop, so reset everything and re-cluster.\\
\textbf{System:} Session reset. Partitioned 288 movies into 13 groups. \quad\textit{[recovered]}\\
\textbf{Oracle:} split the French films by intimate-personal vs societal narrative, into 3 groups.\\
\textbf{System:} Split into 2 sub-clusters: \textsf{Intimate fr}, \textsf{Societal fr}.
  \quad\textit{[asked for 3, produced 2]}
\end{quote}

\noindent The explanation turn was well grounded here (``My Sole Desire'' was correctly placed in
\textsf{Societal fr}). Notably, the judge scored this session's clustering coherence only $2$, and
it visibly looped and returned two groups when three were asked for, yet the oracle rated it $5$.
Oracle satisfaction rewarded responsiveness and recovery over cluster quality.

\paragraph{Failure (\texttt{satire} persona, budget-terminated, unrated).} The oracle wanted to
keep only comedies and group them by tone. The session never converged: it exhausted the 15-turn
budget without the oracle declaring success, and it exhibits three recurring defects at once.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Oracle:} keep only comedy films, then group them.\\
\textbf{System:} Filter matched 74 movies. Split into 2: \textsf{Asian Romantic Comedies Family
  Fantasy Comedies}, \textsf{Family Animated Adventures Family Fantasy Comedies}.\\
\textbf{Oracle:} split these by emotional tone, into 3 groups.\\
\textbf{System:} Split into 4 sub-clusters. \quad\textit{[asked for 3, produced 4]}\\
\textbf{Oracle:} why is \emph{The Graduate} in the \textsf{Biting\,\ldots} group?\\
\textbf{System:} ``A Real Pain'' is placed in this cluster due to\ldots
  \quad\textit{[explains the wrong film]}\\
\textbf{Oracle:} why is \emph{Free Guy} in the \textsf{Low Warm-hearted\,\ldots} group?\\
\textbf{System:} ``Miss Kobayashi's Dragon Maid'' fits this cluster due to\ldots
  \quad\textit{[again the wrong film]}
\end{quote}

\noindent The cluster names also show the labeller \emph{prepending} a tone word to an already
concatenated, inherited label (\textsf{Biting Asian Romantic Comedies Family Fantasy Comedies}),
rather than renaming. These behaviours are quantified as findings in Section~\ref{sec:findings}:
wrong-film explanations, label bloat, and unhonoured split counts.
