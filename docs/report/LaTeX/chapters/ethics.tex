\section{Ethical Considerations}\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{Synthetic oracle limitations.} Every result in this report was produced by an
LLM-simulated oracle, not by human subjects. The findings therefore describe agent-to-agent
dynamics and cannot be assumed to reflect how real users behave, judge, or rate clusters; the
leniency observed in the simulated oracle (Finding~8) underlines that a dedicated human study
would be needed before any claim about human satisfaction.

\paragraph{Model and embedding bias.} The text and visual embedding models, together with the
language models that drive the system, are trained predominantly on Western, English-language
data and carry the cultural biases of that data. As a result, groupings formed along genre,
nationality, or era can encode stereotyped associations, for example treating a region's
cinema as a monolithic ``foreign'' bucket, and the system offers no safeguard against
reproducing them.

\paragraph{Dataset popularity bias.} The underlying catalogue skews heavily toward popular,
mainstream films, both by construction (a popularity threshold is applied during scraping) and by
the nature of the source. This skew propagates into the clusters and the concept axes the system
learns, so less popular traditions and films are under-represented in exactly the structures the
oracle inspects.

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge risks.} Using one language model to evaluate another creates two coupled risks:
shared blind spots, where failure modes both models are prone to go unnoticed, and
self-preference. The design mitigates this by placing the system, the oracle, and the judge on
three different model families, but it does not eliminate the deeper concern that automated
grading can systematically over- or under-value behaviours in correlated ways.
